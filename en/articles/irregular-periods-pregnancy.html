Irregular Periods and Pregnancy: Causes, Ovulation and When to See a Doctor

Educational health guide · Consult a qualified healthcare professional for personal medical advice.

1. What are irregular periods?

Menstrual cycles do not have exactly the same length for everyone. A cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next period. In adults, cycles that are consistently shorter than about 21 days or longer than about 35 days may need medical assessment.

Periods may also be considered irregular when their timing changes significantly from month to month, bleeding is unusually heavy or light, or periods are missed repeatedly. Occasional changes can happen, but a persistent pattern is worth discussing with a healthcare professional.

2. Common causes of irregular periods

Several health and lifestyle factors can affect the hormones that control the menstrual cycle. Common causes include:

  • Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS): PCOS can affect ovulation and may cause infrequent or irregular periods. Some people also experience acne or increased facial and body hair.
  • Thyroid conditions: An underactive or overactive thyroid can affect menstrual cycles.
  • Changes in weight: Significant weight gain or loss may affect hormone balance and ovulation.
  • Stress and intense exercise: Ongoing stress, very strenuous exercise or inadequate nutrition can sometimes disrupt periods.
  • High prolactin levels and other hormonal conditions: These may interfere with normal ovulation.
  • Life stages and medicines: Periods can change during the years after the first period, while approaching menopause, after pregnancy, during breastfeeding or with certain medicines and contraceptives.

These are possible explanations, not a way to diagnose the cause yourself. More than one factor may be involved.

3. Can irregular periods affect pregnancy?

Irregular periods can make it harder to predict when ovulation will happen. If ovulation happens less often or does not happen regularly, there may be fewer opportunities to conceive during a year.

However, irregular periods do not automatically mean that pregnancy is impossible. Some people with irregular cycles conceive naturally. The chances depend on the underlying cause, age and other factors affecting both partners.

4. How can you track ovulation with irregular cycles?

Calendar calculations and period-tracking apps are less dependable when cycle lengths change. You may find it helpful to:

  • Record the first day of each period and how long bleeding lasts.
  • Note changes in cervical mucus. Clear, slippery and stretchy mucus can occur near ovulation, but it cannot confirm ovulation by itself.
  • Discuss urine ovulation predictor kits with a healthcare professional. They detect a rise in luteinising hormone (LH), but results may be harder to interpret in some people with PCOS.
  • Ask whether blood tests or ultrasound monitoring are appropriate to assess ovulation.

Basal body temperature tracking may show a small rise after ovulation, but it usually helps identify a pattern retrospectively rather than predict the exact day.

5. Tips when trying to conceive

  • If possible, have intercourse every two to three days throughout the cycle. This can help when the fertile days are difficult to predict.
  • Discuss preconception health with a qualified healthcare professional.
  • A daily folic acid supplement of 400 micrograms is generally recommended for people planning pregnancy, starting before conception. Some medical conditions or medicines require a different dose, so ask your clinician.
  • Choose balanced meals, regular moderate activity and adequate sleep. These support general health but do not guarantee ovulation or pregnancy.
  • Avoid smoking and recreational drugs. Avoid alcohol when trying to conceive and during pregnancy.
  • Do not take fertility medicines, hormones or supplements advertised as ovulation treatments without medical supervision.

6. What tests might a doctor recommend?

The evaluation depends on your symptoms, health history and how long you have been trying to conceive. A healthcare professional may consider:

  • Review of menstrual history, medicines and other symptoms.
  • Pregnancy testing when a period is missed.
  • Blood tests for thyroid function, prolactin or other hormones when indicated.
  • Assessment for PCOS or other causes of irregular ovulation.
  • Pelvic ultrasound when appropriate.
  • If you are having difficulty conceiving, evaluation of both partners. This may include semen analysis and assessment of the reproductive tract when indicated.

Not everyone needs every test. Your clinician can explain which investigations are suitable for you.

7. When should you see a doctor?

Consider a medical appointment if your periods are repeatedly irregular, you miss periods for several months, bleeding is very heavy, or you have persistent pelvic pain or other concerning symptoms. If you previously had regular periods and they stop for around three months, seek medical advice.

If you are trying to conceive, fertility evaluation is generally recommended after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse when the female partner is under 35, or after 6 months when she is 35 or older. Seek advice sooner if she is over 40 or there are known concerns such as irregular or absent periods, endometriosis or a history of reproductive conditions. You do not need to wait for these time periods if your cycles are persistently irregular.

Seek urgent medical care if you have severe abdominal or pelvic pain, fainting, or very heavy bleeding, especially if pregnancy is possible.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get pregnant if my periods are irregular?

Yes, pregnancy may still be possible. Irregular cycles can make ovulation harder to predict, so medical advice may help identify the cause and suitable next steps.

Does PCOS always cause infertility?

No. PCOS can affect ovulation, but it does not mean pregnancy is impossible. Treatment and support depend on individual circumstances.

Can stress make periods irregular?

Ongoing stress may affect the hormones involved in the menstrual cycle. However, persistent irregular periods should not automatically be blamed on stress; other causes may need assessment.

Should I take medicine to make my periods regular?

Do not start hormonal tablets or fertility medicines on your own. The right approach depends on the cause, your health and whether you are planning pregnancy.

Important note

This article is for general educational purposes only. It does not diagnose menstrual or fertility conditions and is not a substitute for individual medical care. Consult a qualified healthcare professional for advice about irregular periods, ovulation or pregnancy planning.
