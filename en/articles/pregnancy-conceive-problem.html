Difficulty Conceiving: Causes, Tests, Diet and Daily Routine

Educational health guide · Always consult a qualified healthcare professional for personal advice.

1. When can difficulty conceiving be a concern?

Many couples do not conceive immediately. This does not automatically mean that either partner has a fertility problem. Fertility can be affected by factors involving either partner, both partners, or sometimes no clear identifiable cause.

For people trying to conceive, a medical evaluation is generally recommended after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse if the female partner is under 35, or after 6 months if she is 35 or older. People over 40, or anyone with known reproductive health concerns, should seek advice earlier.

2. Common causes

  • Ovulation-related factors: Irregular or absent ovulation, including conditions such as PCOS.
  • Fallopian tube or uterine conditions: Previous infections, endometriosis, fibroids or tubal blockage may affect fertility.
  • Sperm-related factors: Sperm count, movement or shape may affect the chance of pregnancy.
  • Age and health: Fertility changes with age. Some long-term health conditions and certain treatments can also play a role.
  • Unexplained infertility: Sometimes standard tests do not identify a clear cause.

3. Tests a doctor may recommend

The evaluation depends on medical history, age and symptoms. It may include menstrual and health history, assessment of ovulation, blood tests, pelvic ultrasound, and a semen analysis. Tests of the fallopian tubes may be recommended in some cases.

Both partners should be evaluated when appropriate. Not everyone needs every test, and results should be interpreted by a clinician.

4. Food and nutrients while planning pregnancy

  • Choose a varied diet with vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts and suitable protein sources.
  • People planning pregnancy are generally advised to take 400 micrograms of folic acid daily, starting before conception. A clinician may recommend a different dose for some medical conditions or medicines.
  • Stay hydrated and limit highly processed foods where practical.
  • Avoid smoking and recreational drugs. Avoid alcohol when trying to conceive or during pregnancy.
  • Ask a healthcare professional before starting supplements or special fertility diets.

5. Daily routine and lifestyle

Regular moderate physical activity, adequate sleep and a sustainable routine support general health. If cycles are regular, a clinician can explain how to identify the fertile window. Having intercourse every one to two days during that window may help, but tracking should not become a source of pressure.

There is no single food, exercise or home remedy proven to guarantee pregnancy.

6. Medicines and treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. Options may include treating an underlying condition, ovulation-related medicines, surgery in selected situations, or assisted reproductive treatments. Fertility medicines can have side effects and may require monitoring. Do not take hormones, ovulation tablets, antibiotics or other prescription medicines without medical supervision.

7. When to seek medical advice

Consult a qualified doctor if you meet the timeframes above, have very irregular or absent periods, severe pelvic pain, a history of pelvic infection or reproductive surgery, repeated pregnancy loss, or concerns about sperm health. Seek earlier care if you are over 40 or have a known condition that may affect fertility.

Important note

This article is for general education only. It cannot diagnose infertility or replace an individual consultation. Fertility concerns are not one partner's fault; evaluation and support should be respectful and may involve both partners.
