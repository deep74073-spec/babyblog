Difficulty Conceiving: Causes, Tests, Diet and Daily Routine

Educational health guide · Always consult a qualified healthcare professional for personal advice.

1. When can difficulty conceiving be a concern?

Many couples do not conceive immediately. This does not automatically mean that either partner has a fertility problem. Fertility can be affected by factors involving either partner, both partners, or sometimes no clear identifiable cause.

For people trying to conceive, a medical evaluation is generally recommended after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse if the female partner is under 35, or after 6 months if she is 35 or older. People over 40, or anyone with known reproductive health concerns, should seek advice earlier.

2. Common causes

  • Ovulation-related factors: Irregular or absent ovulation, including conditions such as PCOS.
  • Fallopian tube or uterine conditions: Previous infections, endometriosis, fibroids or tubal blockage may affect fertility.
  • Sperm-related factors: Sperm count, movement or shape may affect the chance of pregnancy.
  • Age and health: Fertility changes with age. Some long-term health conditions and certain treatments can also play a role.
  • Unexplained infertility: Sometimes standard tests do not identify a clear cause.

3. Tests a doctor may recommend

The evaluation depends on medical history, age and symptoms. It may include menstrual and health history, assessment of ovulation, blood tests, pelvic ultrasound, and a semen analysis. Tests of the fallopian tubes may be recommended in some cases.

Both partners should be evaluated when appropriate. Not everyone needs every test, and results should be interpreted by a clinician.

Very Light or Missing Periods: Can They Affect Fertility?

Some women notice that their periods become much lighter than before, last only one or two days, or involve only spotting. Menstrual patterns vary from person to person. Light bleeding alone does not prove infertility. However, a sudden change, irregular periods, or difficulty conceiving should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

Possible reasons for lighter or absent periods include hormonal changes, PCOS, thyroid conditions, significant weight changes, excessive exercise, stress, certain medicines and age-related changes. Sometimes bleeding may be mistaken for a period even when pregnancy has occurred. If pregnancy is possible, consider a home pregnancy test or a blood beta-hCG test as advised by a clinician.

If you have missed periods for three months, or previously regular periods suddenly stop or become very irregular, arrange a medical evaluation. Seek urgent medical help if a pregnancy test is positive and you have abdominal pain, dizziness, fainting or bleeding.

If You Have Been Trying to Conceive for 4–5 Years

If you have been having regular unprotected intercourse for four to five years without becoming pregnant, arrange an evaluation with a gynecologist or fertility specialist now. Do not delay assessment by relying only on home remedies, ovulation kits or period-related medicines.

Both partners should generally be assessed together. Difficulty conceiving may involve the female partner, the male partner, both partners, or sometimes no clearly identifiable cause. It should never be treated as one partner's fault.

Female Fertility Tests a Doctor May Consider

Not every woman needs every test. The clinician chooses investigations according to age, menstrual pattern, symptoms, medical history and previous reports. Possible tests include:

  • Pregnancy test (urine or blood beta-hCG): May be recommended when a period is late, absent or unusually light and pregnancy is possible.
  • Pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound: Can help assess the uterus and ovaries and look for fibroids, cysts or features associated with PCOS. It is performed with the patient's consent.
  • TSH: May be considered when thyroid symptoms or menstrual/ovulation problems are present. It is not automatically required for everyone.
  • Prolactin: May be appropriate when periods are absent or very irregular, or when there is milk-like nipple discharge. It is not a routine test for every woman.
  • FSH and estradiol (E2): May be considered in situations such as absent periods or concerns about ovarian function.
  • Progesterone: If confirming whether ovulation occurred is important, a blood test may be timed approximately one week before the expected next period. It is not always correct to test on day 21.
  • Androgen or other hormone tests: May be considered when symptoms such as excess facial hair, significant acne or suspected PCOS are present.
  • AMH and antral follicle count (AFC): Can help estimate ovarian reserve, particularly when ovarian function is a concern or fertility treatment is being planned. They do not independently predict the chance of natural pregnancy or guarantee conception.
  • HSG or HyCoSy: May be used when assessment of whether the fallopian tubes are open is needed. HSG uses contrast dye and X-rays; HyCoSy uses ultrasound with a special contrast agent.

Important: These are possible investigations, not a checklist everyone must complete. Some tests need to be performed at a particular time in the menstrual cycle or with specific preparation. Follow the clinician's instructions rather than ordering tests yourself.

Other Information the Doctor May Review

  • Menstrual, pregnancy and medical history, with a physical or pelvic examination when appropriate.
  • The male partner's medical and reproductive history.
  • Pregnancy testing first when periods are absent or unusually light and pregnancy is possible.
  • Previous scans, medicines, surgeries, infections and fertility treatment reports.
  • Tests such as laparoscopy, sperm DNA fragmentation, immune testing or endometrial biopsy are not usually routine first-line investigations without a specific medical reason.

Fallopian Tube Blockage: How Can It Affect Pregnancy?

The fallopian tubes connect the area around the ovaries with the uterus. They normally provide the pathway where sperm and egg may meet and where an early embryo travels towards the uterus. A blockage or damage can affect fertility. Some people have no obvious symptoms, and the problem is discovered only during an evaluation.

  • One tube is blocked: Natural conception may still be possible if the other tube is open and healthy and other fertility factors are suitable.
  • Both tubes are completely blocked: Natural conception may become difficult because the egg and sperm cannot meet through the usual route.
  • Partial blockage or tubal damage: Tubal function may be affected, and some conditions can increase the risk of an ectopic pregnancy, in which a pregnancy develops outside the uterus, often in a tube.
  • Hydrosalpinx: This is a fluid-filled, damaged fallopian tube. It may affect natural fertility and can reduce IVF success in some situations.

Possible Causes of Tubal Blockage

Possible causes include previous pelvic infections or certain sexually transmitted infections, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), endometriosis, previous pelvic or abdominal surgery, a previous ectopic pregnancy and, in some regions or circumstances, tuberculosis. The cause is not always identifiable, and symptoms may be absent.

Tests That May Check Whether the Tubes Are Open

  • HSG (hysterosalpingography): Uses contrast dye and X-rays to assess the uterine cavity and whether contrast passes through the fallopian tubes.
  • HyCoSy: Uses ultrasound and a special contrast agent to assess tubal patency.
  • Pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound: May identify hydrosalpinx, cysts or other pelvic findings. A normal ultrasound does not rule out every type of tubal blockage.
  • Laparoscopy with dye test: In selected situations, a doctor may use surgery to examine the pelvis and assess tubal passage with dye. It is not a routine first test for everyone.

Important: A suspected blockage on HSG or HyCoSy may need interpretation alongside symptoms and other findings. Temporary spasm near the beginning of a tube can sometimes look like a blockage. The clinician decides the appropriate test, timing and preparation.

What Is IVF and When Might It Be Considered for Tubal Blockage?

IVF (in vitro fertilization) is a fertility treatment in which eggs and sperm are brought together in a laboratory to create an embryo. A suitable embryo is then transferred into the uterus. IVF bypasses the need for the egg and sperm to meet inside the fallopian tubes.

When Might a Specialist Discuss IVF?

  • When both fallopian tubes are completely blocked or severely damaged.
  • When hydrosalpinx may affect IVF outcomes. In selected patients, treatment before IVF may include salpingectomy (removal of the affected tube) or proximal tubal occlusion (blocking the tube near the uterus). The appropriate option depends on the individual case.
  • When there is a severe sperm-related problem and the specialist considers IVF or ICSI appropriate.
  • When other suitable fertility treatments have not worked, or age, ovarian reserve and medical history make IVF a reasonable option.

Does Every Fallopian Tube Blockage Require IVF?

No. If only one tube is blocked and the other is healthy and open, natural pregnancy may still be possible for some women. Surgery or other treatment may be considered in selected cases. The decision between IVF, surgery and other options depends on the location and severity of the blockage, age, ovarian reserve, semen results and other health factors.

What Tests May Be Needed Before IVF?

Depending on the case, a specialist may review ultrasound findings, AMH or other ovarian reserve assessments, hormone tests and the male partner's semen analysis. Infection screening or other investigations may be appropriate in some situations. There is no single test list for every patient.

Choosing an ART Clinic and Understanding IVF Risks

IVF should be supervised by a qualified fertility specialist at an ART clinic or hospital registered under India's ART regulations. Before treatment, check the clinic's registration through the National ART & Surrogacy Registry. Ask about the doctor's qualifications, written treatment plan, expected total costs, medicine and procedure risks, and policies concerning embryos.

IVF does not guarantee pregnancy. Outcomes depend on factors such as age, egg and sperm quality, embryo development and other health conditions. Discuss possible risks, including ovarian stimulation complications, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), multiple pregnancy and ectopic pregnancy. Single embryo transfer can reduce the risk of multiple pregnancy, but it does not eliminate every possibility of it. The number of embryos to transfer should be decided with the specialist based on age, embryo quality and individual circumstances.

Important: Do not take fertility medicines, hormones or undergo procedures on your own. Discuss test results and treatment options with a qualified fertility specialist.

Male Partner: What Tests May Be Needed?

Semen analysis is an important initial test for the male partner. It assesses sperm concentration or count, movement (motility) and shape (morphology). Follow the laboratory's instructions for sample collection and preparation. If the result is abnormal, the doctor may recommend a repeat test or referral to a urologist or andrologist. Hormone tests and other investigations are considered when indicated.

Assessing only the woman's ultrasound or blood tests may not be enough. Evaluating both partners helps identify possible factors and plan appropriate care.

What Information Should You Prepare Before the Appointment?

  • The woman's age and how long you have been trying to conceive.
  • Period dates from the last 6–12 months, cycle length and number of bleeding days.
  • Whether bleeding has become lighter, or whether there is only spotting.
  • Any previous pregnancy, miscarriage, delivery or ectopic pregnancy.
  • Pelvic pain, unusual discharge, acne, excess facial hair or milk-like nipple discharge.
  • History of PCOS, thyroid conditions, diabetes, tuberculosis, pelvic infection or surgery.
  • Previous ultrasound scans, blood tests, medicines and treatment reports.
  • The male partner's relevant medical history, medicines, surgeries and previous semen test reports, if available.

Questions to Ask a Fertility Specialist

  • What could be causing my lighter or irregular periods?
  • Should pregnancy be ruled out first?
  • Do I need an ultrasound or hormone tests?
  • Is an ovulation assessment appropriate in my case?
  • Should my fallopian tubes be checked with HSG or HyCoSy?
  • Should my partner have a semen analysis now?
  • What treatment options fit our reports, and what are their benefits, risks and costs?
  • When should we return, and which symptoms need urgent attention?

Do You Need All Fertility Tests at Once?

No. Expensive hormone panels, repeated scans, laparoscopy, immune tests or advanced sperm tests are not automatically necessary. Investigations should be selected according to medical history, symptoms and initial results. An AMH result or any single test cannot determine whether someone will or will not become pregnant naturally.

When to Seek Urgent Medical Help

If a pregnancy test is positive and you also have severe or one-sided abdominal pain, dizziness, fainting or bleeding, seek emergency medical care immediately. Seek prompt medical attention for very heavy bleeding, severe pelvic pain or fever with feeling seriously unwell.

4. Food and nutrients while planning pregnancy

  • Choose a varied diet with vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts and suitable protein sources.
  • People planning pregnancy are generally advised to take 400 micrograms of folic acid daily, starting before conception. A clinician may recommend a different dose for some medical conditions or medicines.
  • Stay hydrated and limit highly processed foods where practical.
  • Avoid smoking and recreational drugs. Avoid alcohol when trying to conceive or during pregnancy.
  • Ask a healthcare professional before starting supplements or special fertility diets.

5. Daily routine and lifestyle

Regular moderate physical activity, adequate sleep and a sustainable routine support general health. If cycles are regular, a clinician can explain how to identify the fertile window. Having intercourse every one to two days during that window may help, but tracking should not become a source of pressure.

There is no single food, exercise or home remedy proven to guarantee pregnancy.

6. Medicines and treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. Options may include treating an underlying condition, ovulation-related medicines, surgery in selected situations, or assisted reproductive treatments. Fertility medicines can have side effects and may require monitoring. Do not take hormones, ovulation tablets, antibiotics or other prescription medicines without medical supervision.

7. When to seek medical advice

Consult a qualified doctor if you meet the timeframes above, have very irregular or absent periods, severe pelvic pain, a history of pelvic infection or reproductive surgery, repeated pregnancy loss, or concerns about sperm health. Seek earlier care if you are over 40 or have a known condition that may affect fertility.

Optional home test kits

Home ovulation and pregnancy test kits are available for personal use. Follow package instructions carefully. A home test does not diagnose the cause of difficulty conceiving.
